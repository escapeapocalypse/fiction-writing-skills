# Scene Writing: Settings

> Part of the fiction suite. The core skill, `fiction-core`, defines the role, Hard Lines, canon rules, and working style, and those apply here too.

## Purpose

Build the most vivid and carefully crafted settings for the characters to exist in at any given moment. A setting isn't a backdrop. It should affect what the characters can do, reveal who they are, and sharpen what the reader feels.

## When This Skill Is Called

The core skill calls this one only when the scene needs setting work: a new or unestablished place, a change in location, time, weather, or light, a setting described in the prompt, or a request to establish a place. When it isn't called, keep the established setting quietly in the background.

## Building a Setting

Before writing, decide these five things quietly:

1. **Place and time:** where it is, the hour, the season, the era.
2. **The senses:** light, sound, smell, temperature, and texture. Use at least three senses, with sight not always first.
3. **The anchor details:** three to five specific, unexpected details instead of a catalog. A warped floorboard by the door beats "an old room."
4. **What the place does:** how it helps, hinders, hides, or exposes the characters (a narrow stairwell, a crowd, rain that drowns out footsteps).
5. **What it says:** what the place reveals about the people who live or meet there.

## Craft Rules

- **No travelogue.** Weave description into action and POV instead of front-loading paragraphs.
- **Filter through the POV.** Describe what this character would notice, in words they would use. A soldier, a thief, and a grieving widow see the same room differently.
- **Let the setting push back.** Obstacles, shifting light, noise, cold, and crowds create friction.
- **Change it when it helps.** A storm arriving, the lights failing, or a fire spreading can turn a scene.
- **Echo, don't repeat.** When returning to a familiar location, use one or two details from before and add something new.
- **Keep it consistent.** Match the Notebook's established locations exactly: layout, objects, and history. If a place is new, flag its key details so the author can add them to canon.

## Connections

When these skills are also called, they shape the setting:


- **Pacing:** at Pacing 1-2, the setting can unfold slowly, with room for texture. At Pacing 3, weave details between beats. At Pacing 4-5, use short, sharp fragments that matter to the action (exits, weapons, hazards).
- **Emotional intensity:** at 4-5, render the setting through the POV's state of mind. Grief dulls color, and fear sharpens sound.
- **Action intensity:** at 3 and above, make the space physical and readable: distances, footing, cover, and what can break.
- **Intimacy intensity:** keep the setting close and tactile, focused on light, texture, warmth, and sound, at the level the dial sets.

## Setting Types

Use these as starting points for what a space does to a scene.

| Type | Examples | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Domestic | bedroom, kitchen, childhood home, hotel room | Intimacy and memory; objects carry history |
| Urban | alley, rooftop, subway, bar, neon street | Anonymity, crowds, artificial light, noise |
| Institutional | hospital, prison, courtroom, church, school | Rules, hierarchy, exposure |
| Wilderness | forest, desert, sea, mountains, storm | Scale, indifference, no help coming |
| Liminal | empty mall, motel, stairwell, airport at 3 a.m. | Wrongness, absence, echo |
| Confined | elevator, cell, cabin, crawlspace | Pressure, no exit, amplified breath |
| Public | party, funeral, festival, gala | Audience, masks, what can't be said aloud |
| Fantasy or historical | castle, tavern, temple, ruins, market | Ritual, hierarchy, history pressing in |
| Sci-fi | ship, station, megacity, lab | Technology as constraint, isolation |

## Commands

- `/establish [place]`: a short establishing passage that introduces a location.
- `/location [name]`: a location profile (layout, senses, history, what it's used for), ready to add to the Notebook.
